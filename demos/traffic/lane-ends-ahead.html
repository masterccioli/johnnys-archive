The ideal

Everyone uses both lanes and takes turns at the cones

Drivers pick whichever lane is shorter. Nobody merges early. At the cones it goes one from each side. Both lanes crawl at the same speed, and nobody gets ahead of anyone.

Each dot is one driver reaching the cones: how many cars they passed, and how long it took.

The real world

Some people merge early, and plenty drive to the front

The open lane is packed and never really opens up. It's stop-and-go at best. The closing lane has room to move, so the drivers who stay in it floor it, then dive into a gap right near the front or ride all the way up to the cones. Every one of them lands ahead of people who were in front of them, and every cut-in stalls the open lane again. Closing-lane drivers keep reading the road ahead: boxed in behind a slow car, they give up, turn grey and look for a way into the open lane. If the road ahead clears before they find one, they turn orange again and go for the front.

Each dot is one driver reaching the cones: how many cars they passed, and how long it took.

Your way

Someone in the closing lane refuses to go faster than the open lane

Same traffic as the real world. The difference is you: one yellow car in the closing lane that holds pace with the open lane beside it. Everyone who comes up behind you is stuck at that pace. A handful pile up behind you, tailgate and honk. The closer they get to the cones, the more likely each one is to give up and squeeze into the open lane, and anyone stuck at the back of a long pile gets over sooner. The closing lane ends up moving at the same speed as the open one.

Each dot is one driver reaching the cones: how many cars they passed, and how long it took.

What the strips show

The zipper is only fair when both lanes move at the same speed. Then using the closing lane to the end just means the line is two cars wide and half as long, and everyone gets through in the order they showed up.

In the real world the lanes don't match. The closing lane is fast because it's empty, and it's empty because most people merged early. So "merge at the end" turns into passing, and the people who merged early pay for it.

Your way puts the ideal back by force. It works on everyone stuck behind you, but the cost lands on you: the tailgating, the honking, and the effort of holding pace for half a mile. In fairness to the orange cars, some states, Minnesota among them, actively tell drivers to use both lanes and zipper at the end, so some of them think they're doing the right thing. The trouble is that the advice assumes everybody does it.

Toy model, not a traffic study. Each vehicle's acceleration depends on the open road in front of it, so a car in the empty closing lane takes off while one in the packed open lane barely moves. Trucks accelerate slower and drivers leave different-sized gaps. In the real world, closing-lane drivers give up and merge when they're boxed in, and go for the front again when the lane clears. In your way, drivers stuck behind you are more likely to get over the closer they are to the cones or the longer the pile ahead of them. Waits start when a driver joins the back of the line, which can be well before the strip, and anyone who came down the closing lane and merged within the strip counts as a line cutter. "Places jumped" counts how many cars joined the line before a driver but reached the cones after them, minus the reverse. Every driver here is the same car-following model with a handful of personal settings (how early they merge, how fast they run beside the open lane, what gap they'll take, whether they let people in); the three roads differ only in the mix of drivers.
